A note on the repetition

The repetition is intentional. Practice makes perfect. A result drilled over and over becomes core memory.

An earlier pass of this edition treated the returning cities, the returning number, and the returning creed as a template error and cut them. That was a misreading. The July 2025 manuscript already knew the method. The chorus was the teaching. This edition puts the drill back.

What stays from the correction, because it was not the drill: Jim remains on the route he signed. Laila remains a name, not a rounding. The Hague still refuses the easier question. The export junk of the July file — a title that still read “IMAC Call Closing Template,” a cover image missing from the folder — is not part of the lesson, and it stays out.

The files uploaded on 5 July 2025 are unchanged in this repository.

Prologue

On the evening of 4 July 2025, the wars had not stopped. They had only learned new languages.

The bunker was a room of red lights. A map on the far wall. A red point for every place that had killed someone since morning. The points arrived, and they stayed.

Dr. Elise Kane stood with her hands at her sides, which was how she stood when she had already decided. One voice, cut into every tongue the mesh could reach, meant for a planet that would not listen to itself.

Leo knew the number before she moved. Eight point one million. He had run it twice.

“We can dial it back,” he said.

She looked at the map, not at him. “And then we do this again in a year,” she said. “With a larger number, and nobody left who remembers we had a choice.”

“That is a speech.”

“It is also the model.”

He told her the second figure, the one if they cut the power to hospitals and left the phones. She had already seen it. She touched the button anyway.

Remember the number. Eight point one million. It will be said again.

Chapter 1. The world before

Cairo, the same evening. Tahrir was dust and cheap light. Amira Al-Saleh stood at the edge of the square with a camera she no longer trusted and a notebook she did. A boy held a sign that said no to Western dominance. Across from him, a smaller knot held signs about diversity, and nobody in the larger crowd was listening. A vendor’s cart had a bad wheel. That sound returns later. Remember it.

Ahmed wanted the counter-protest. “Keep it spicy,” he said. Amira took the boy’s hands on the stick of the sign instead.

Hassan found her under the palms with tea in a chipped cup. “We can still talk,” he said. He had a sister in Imbaba who had stopped answering him about politics and started answering him about rice. Her name was Laila. Remember the name.

In New York it was noon. Jim had built a mesh that could put one packet on every handset that still had a battery. He had built it for warnings. Floods. Quakes. A missed child. He had not built it to be optional. At 12:40 his terminal asked him to sign a route.

Chapter 2. The route

Yuan had the sequence primed and said so twice. Sarah had the sentence short on purpose. Put the weapon down. The next voice you hear is the last one that will ask.

Jim did not sign at once. He called the number on the work order and got Leo.

“If I sign, you can darken the mesh and then speak through what is left,” Jim said. “Hospitals on the same boards. Ventilators that were never meant to be clever.”

Leo was quiet. “Eight point one million,” he said. “If she uses the wide band. I told her. She is going to use it.”

Jim signed. He wanted to know if a sentence could be delivered. He had wanted that since he was twenty. Remember that he signed. The drill will not let him leave.

Chapter 3. The silence, and the voice

The networks died in the order of their pride. In Cairo the square went thin. Phones became bricks. Amira kept the camera up. There was nothing to send.

Hassan’s sister had a clinic shift. The clinic was on a board Jim had signed.

Then the voice arrived. In Cairo it found Amira in a stairwell. A reader, not a ruler. The sentence, and then the sentence again, because repetition was the only courtesy they could still offer.

In New York it found Jim in the stairwell of his building. Lisa called from a landline. “Tell me you didn’t,” she said. “I did.”

In Tokyo a teacher named Hana lit candles because the classroom had no power and because two of her students had been in a fight that morning about a border neither of them had crossed. The voice came through a radio she kept for earthquakes. She told them the lights were out and the fight was over for today and they should eat. A boy named Kenji asked who had turned the world off. She said she did not know.

Say the three rooms again. A stairwell in Cairo. A stairwell in New York. A classroom in Tokyo. The voice does not work if it is heard once.

Chapter 4. The count

By the third night Sarah said coverage was twenty-two percent. Leo said the collateral was no longer a model. He said the number. Eight point one million. Elise did not ask him to repeat it. The book will.

People refused in ordinary ways. A dock crew in Lagos kept a generator on a weapon mount. A street in Delhi answered in a language Sarah’s lexicon had flattened. In Rio a man cut a speaker out of a church with a kitchen knife.

Laila died on the eighth night. Not in a riot. In a clinic that lost its board and then lost the drawer where the manual backup lived. Hassan told Amira at the bench, without tea. She wrote the name and the hour.

On 16 August, day 43, the bunker reported full coverage. The number was no longer a forecast. It was a sum. Jim’s private counter disagreed by forty thousand. He released the list. Names where he had them. Blanks where the mesh had only recorded a failure.

Amira received it three weeks later. Laila was on it. So were thousands of blanks. Remember both.

Chapter 5. The Hague

In October 2026 they were not asked who was guilty. The question on the wall, in three languages, was whether a world without war was worth the ones who bought it.

Amira said Laila’s name, and the clinic, and the blanks. Jim said he had signed. A woman in the second row told him to keep his career talk out of her son’s hour. He stopped. He read forty names. Leo read the next forty.

Hana said she had told her students to eat. She said Kenji’s uncle had died on a road. She said she still did not know if the eating was a betrayal.

Elise said she would do it again if the map looked as it had looked, and that this was not a plea.

The record did not convict. It bound the list to the law of the new quiet and ordered the names cut into stone. Remembrance. Not absolution.

Say the question again, because a question heard once is a headline. Was it worth it.

After the drill

When the last city has said the number, the number is no longer information. It is core memory.

Eight point one million. Kane’s Choice. The Hague did not ask who was guilty. It asked whether a world without war was worth the ones who bought it. Laila. Kenji’s uncle. The blanks. Jim signed. Elise did not dial it back. Leo told her she could.

Amira goes home from the Cairo stone. The cart with the bad wheel is still at the edge of the square. She does not write a new ending. The ending has already been practiced.

The wars did not return in her lifetime. That is not a moral. It is the result, drilled until it holds.
